Each design says what it can show about a student and where that evidence stops. What you can say about a student comes from the set of tasks across a topic and program, so combine designs whose evidence stops in different places.

3Make it yoursBriefs and rubrics are starting points, not scripts.

Change the wording, the task, the timings and the content to fit your outcomes, students and practice. Keep the core of the design, which each entry names, because that is what makes the evidence hold.

The six College capabilities

A soft signal. Some designs also build these, and you do not need to assess them. Open one to see what it means.
1Judging quality

Judging whether work is good enough for its purpose against a stated standard, whether it is their own, someone else’s, or work a tool produced.

Is this good enough for the purpose, and by what standard?

Also called evaluative judgement. A design is marked as building it when the student judges the quality of work, their own, someone else’s or a tool’s, against a stated standard, and that judgement is marked.

14 of 66 designs build this
2Testing claims

Examining the evidence, assumptions and reasoning behind a claim before relying on it, including claims in what a tool returns, however confident they sound.

Is this claim actually supported?

Also called critical thinking. A design is marked as building it when the student’s reasoning about a claim is visible and something in the task tests it: a challenge, a check against a source, or a result that can turn out wrong. Checking what a tool returns counts here.

35 of 66 designs build this
3Acting responsibly

Deciding whether a tool or other resource should be used for a piece of work, and if so how, having weighed the effects on the people it could reach, and being prepared to answer for that decision. It covers effects the student can foresee and act on within the task, such as privacy, safety and honesty about how the work was produced.

Should this be used here, who could it affect, and can I stand behind it?

Also called ethical reasoning. A design is marked as building it when the student decides whether and how a tool, source or other resource is used, weighs foreseeable effects on people such as privacy, safety or honesty about how the work was produced, and that decision is marked and answerable.

2 of 66 designs build this
4Knowing and using the tools

Knowing which tools and information systems are available for a task, what each can and cannot do, and what happens to information put into it, then using the chosen one competently.

Do I know what’s available for this, and am I using it well?

Also called digital literacy. A design is marked as building it when the student chooses or uses a tool or information system, or works out what one can and cannot do, and that shows in the evidence. Producing a digital artefact does not count on its own.

10 of 66 designs build this
5Showing how the work was done

Deciding what stays with them and what goes to other people, sources or tools, and leaving a trail someone else could follow.

Could someone see how this work came together, and why it was divided that way?

Also called distributed cognition. A design is marked as building it when the path the work took through people, sources and tools is visible in what is submitted.

18 of 66 designs build this
6Staying in charge of your own thinking

Noticing how people, sources and tools are shaping their reasoning, committing to a position before leaning on them, and stopping, checking or referring when they reach the edge of what they can safely judge.

What did I think before I asked, and is this still my judgement?

Also called hybrid metacognition. A design is marked as building it when the student commits to a position before leaning on others, or has to stop, check, escalate or refer, and that is observable in the evidence.

17 of 66 designs build this

Using the Flinders AI Assessment Scale

The scale has six levels. Choose the level that fits what the task is for: ask what you need to see the student do themselves, then pick the level that still lets you see it. Under each level, the wording in the box is the Flinders wording for students, to use as written. The notes on when it fits and how to mark it are practice guidance from this repository.

An unsupervised task cannot be AI-free.No AI permitted can only be relied on where the assessed part is supervised or observed. On an unsupervised task it is a statement you cannot check, so what you mark has to hold up whatever tool was used. The declaration, drafts and appendix required at every other level are what let you see how AI was used.

No AI permitted

Relied on only where supervised or observedSuggested for 30 designs, for the live or supervised part
For this assessment task, students are not permitted to use Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the development of their submission.

Students provide

Nothing extra.

Practice note: when it fits

Only where the assessed part is supervised or observed: an exam, a test in class, a viva, a skills station. On an unsupervised task this is a statement you cannot check, so it is not offered for unsupervised parts in this repository.

Practice note: marking

Mark as usual. Say in the brief whether students may prepare with AI beforehand, since preparation is not supervised.

AI-Prompted

Any task, including unsupervisedSuggested for 13 designs
For this assessment task, students are permitted to use in-built generative AI such as slide design or grammatical/editing but are required to generate their own responses in line with conditions and expectations of the task outlined in the instructions. Students must acknowledge AI suggestions that were acted on/incorporated into the final submission. Students are required to acknowledge how they used AI in their final submission.

How should the use of AI be acknowledged in the assessment task?
Students must provide a declaration acknowledging which AI technologies have been used and how. This should be included at the beginning of the submission. Drafts should be kept demonstrating authorship and may be requested by assessors. An appendix containing all prompts and output generated from AI should be included with the final submission.

For example: I acknowledge the use of ChatGPT in suggested grammar and spelling (OpenAI, Year). The prompts and output from ChatGPT are included in Appendix 1.

A reference to the use of AI should be included in the reference list.
For example: (Year). ChatGPT (Month, Year, Version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat

Students provide

A declaration at the start, an appendix of all prompts and output, acknowledgement of AI suggestions acted on, and a reference list entry. Drafts kept and available on request.

Practice note: when it fits

Students will work in software with AI features built in, such as slide design or grammar suggestions, and the response itself has to be their own.

Practice note: marking

Mark the student’s own response. The declaration and appendix show which suggestions were taken up.

AI-Assisted Idea Generation & Structuring

Any task, including unsupervisedSuggested for 21 designs
For this assessment task, students can use Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the development of their submission. Students are permitted to use AI to brainstorm ideas or provide ideas on structuring of documents. They cannot create content using AI (e.g. sentences, paragraphs, images etc). Students are required to provide AI prompts used and drafts of their work and acknowledge how they used AI in their final submission.

How should the use of AI be acknowledged in the assessment task?
Students must provide a declaration acknowledging which AI technologies have been used and how. This should be included at the beginning of the submission. Drafts should be kept demonstrating authorship and may be requested by assessors. An appendix containing all prompts and output generated from AI should be included with the final submission.

For example: I acknowledge the use of ChatGPT in the early stages of brainstorming the research ideas for this project (OpenAI, Year). The prompts and output from ChatGPT are included in Appendix 1.

A reference to the use of AI should be included in the reference list.
For example: (Year). ChatGPT (Month, Year, Version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat

Students provide

AI prompts and drafts of their work, a declaration at the start, an appendix of all prompts and output, and a reference list entry.

Practice note: when it fits

Getting started or planning is where help is fine, and the content and reasoning are what you are assessing.

Practice note: marking

Mark the content, which has to be the student’s own. Use the drafts and prompts to see how the work developed, and ask about it where the draft and the final version do not line up.

AI-Task Completion, Human Evaluation

Any task, including unsupervisedSuggested for 3 designs
For this assessment task, students are permitted to use AI for some or most elements of the task and demonstrate effective use of AI. They are expected to evaluate output irrespective of how it is attained, include all prompts and output in an appendix, and acknowledge and cite AI created content in their final submission.

How should the use of AI be acknowledged in the assessment task?
Students must provide a declaration acknowledging which AI technologies have been used and how. This should be included at the beginning of the submission. Drafts should be kept demonstrating authorship and may be requested by assessors. An appendix containing all prompts and output generated from AI should be included with the final submission.

For example: I acknowledge the use of ChatGPT in generating content included in this submission (OpenAI, Year). The prompts and output from ChatGPT are included in Appendix 1.

A reference to the use of AI should be included in the reference list as well as in-text.
For example: (Year). ChatGPT (Month, Year, Version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat

Students provide

All prompts and output in an appendix, AI-created content cited in-text and in the reference list, and a declaration at the start.

Practice note: when it fits

The skill you want to see is evaluating and correcting output, for example appraising an AI-drafted care plan against evidence and the person’s context.

Practice note: marking

Mark the evaluation of the output. The product on its own tells you little about the student.

Full AI

Any task, including unsupervisedNot suggested for any design in the repository
For this assessment task, students are expected to use AI creatively and collaboratively to complete the task and demonstrate effective use of AI. They are expected to evaluate output, keep all prompts and output, and acknowledge and cite AI created content in their final submission.

How should the use of AI be acknowledged in the assessment task?
Students must provide a declaration acknowledging which AI technologies have been used and how. This should be included at the beginning of the submission. Drafts should be kept demonstrating authorship and may be requested by assessors. An appendix containing all prompts and output generated from AI should be included with the final submission.

For example: I acknowledge the use of ChatGPT in generating content included in this submission (OpenAI, Year). The prompts and output from ChatGPT are included in Appendix 1.

A reference to the use of AI should be included in the reference list as well as in-text.
For example: (Year). ChatGPT (Month, Year, Version) [Large language model]. https://chat.openai.com/chat

Students provide

All prompts and output kept, AI-created content cited in-text and in the reference list, and a declaration at the start.

Practice note: when it fits

Using AI well is part of the outcome itself, such as capstone or professional work.

Practice note: marking

Mark the evaluation and the judgement shown. If the task carries a high-stakes judgement about the student, pair it with a live or supervised element.

Two things that apply at every levelAssistive technology agreed through an access plan sits outside these levels, and no student should need to reveal a disability to comply. A declaration of AI use tells you what happened. It is not, on its own, evidence that learning occurred.

AI assessment strategies

The University expects each assessment task to carry at least two of these strategies. Each one below is set out as the University describes it, with the designs in this repository that use it. Open a design to see how it carries the strategy in practice.

Plagiarism and AI detection tools, and digital forensics and platform analytics, are applied to a submission rather than built into a design, so few designs list them.

Progressive submission deadlines

Used by 2 designs. Students submit components (e.g. proposal, outline, draft, final) across time, making learning and decision‑making visible.

What it is

Students submit components (e.g. proposal, outline, draft, final) across time, making learning and decision‑making visible.

Breaks the task into staged artefacts (proposal → outline → draft → final) to make learning visible over time.

Why it matters for AI

Supports appropriate AI use for idea generation and structuring while limiting last‑minute, fully AI‑generated artefacts.

Best fit on the scale: AI-Assisted Idea Generation & Structuring to AI-Task Completion, Human Evaluation (also works No AI permitted to AI-Prompted)

Example

In a health sciences literature review, students submit a topic rationale (Week 3), AI‑assisted outline with prompts disclosed (Week 5), and a final critical synthesis (Week 8). Marks are distributed across stages.

Designs in the repository that use it: Triple jump, Patchwork assessment

What the Assessment Design Repository is based on

The repository draws on eleven sources that set the terms most academics will meet. Two of them argue with each other, and that disagreement is stated here because it explains how the repository sorts its designs. Each entry says what the source argues and why it matters for your assessment decisions.

The rules we work within

1 source
Higher Education Standards Framework, sections 1.4.3 and 1.4.4Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021Open ↗
What it argues

Section 1.4.3 requires assessment methods that can confirm all specified learning outcomes are achieved, with grades that reflect the level of attainment. Section 1.4.4 requires that students have demonstrated the course outcomes on completion, "whether assessed at unit level, course level, or in combination".

Why it matters

The words "or in combination" allow a program to build its evidence across several tasks. No single topic has to carry every outcome, so the evidence can sit where it can actually be gathered.

Sector guidance

3 sources
Assessment reform for the age of artificial intelligenceLodge, Howard, Bearman, Dawson and colleagues, TEQSA, 2023Open ↗
What it argues

Two principles and five propositions. The principles are that assessment should prepare students to take part ethically and actively in a society where AI is everywhere, and that trustworthy judgements about students need several inclusive, contextual approaches. The propositions call for authentic engagement with AI, a program-wide approach, attention to the process of learning, students working appropriately with each other and with AI, and security at meaningful points across a program.

Why it matters

Proposition five is the answer when someone argues that every task must be secured. Security belongs at the points that inform progression and completion decisions.

Enacting assessment reform in a time of artificial intelligenceLodge, Bearman, Dawson, Gniel, Harper, Liu, McLean, Ucnik and colleagues, TEQSA, September 2025Open ↗
What it argues

The practical follow-up to the 2023 paper. It sets out three institutional pathways: program-wide redesign, a secure task in every subject, or a mix of the two. It names medical education's programmatic approach as an example and names oral assessment as a workable format for the subject-level pathway.

Why it matters

The three pathways cost very different amounts to run, and the marking time on each design in the repository lets you price them.

TEQSA paper on adaptive capabilitiesTEQSA, June 2026Open ↗
What it argues

Names adaptive capabilities that carry across contexts whatever the tool, reported as evaluative judgement, critical thinking, ethical reasoning and metacognitive regulation. Its central argument is that handing thinking over to tools weakens a student's ability to regulate their own thinking, and its proposed response is evidence of process assessed alongside the final product: drafts, version history, what was tried and dropped, and how the student responded to feedback.

Why it matters

The College's six capabilities are adapted from this paper rather than copied. The repository notes which of them each design can also build, as a light signal rather than something you need to assess. The process evidence designs in the repository put its main recommendation into practice. Check the exact wording against the paper before you quote it.

Models from other institutions

3 sources
The two-lane approachBridgeman, Weeks and Liu, University of Sydney, 2024 to 2026Open ↗
What it argues

Lane 1 is secured assessment of learning, usually at program or stage level, confirming that outcomes are met. Lane 2 is open assessment for and as learning, mostly at subject level, where AI use is scaffolded and supported. The model links itself directly to the Higher Education Standards Framework.

Why it matters

It is the clearest worked model and the vocabulary most colleagues will already know. A task sits in a lane because of the schedule around it, so the same design can serve either lane.

Programmatic assessmentVan der Vleuten, Schuwirth, Driessen and colleagues, Medical Teacher, 2012Open ↗
What it argues

Each individual assessment is designed for learning and feedback, while high-stakes decisions rest on many assessments taken together. Decisions are made through periodic review of the combined evidence, with expert judgement made trustworthy through sampling and careful process.

Why it matters

It is the source of the idea that one assessment is weak evidence on its own, and it is a tradition health programs already recognise.

Where the rubber meets the roadSchut, Maggio, Heeneman, van Tartwijk, van der Vleuten and Driessen, Perspectives on Medical Education, 2021Open ↗
What it argues

A review of 27 studies of programmatic assessment in practice. It finds value in drawing on several sources of evidence for each area of competence, and in matching the amount of evidence to the stakes of the decision. It also reports persistent problems with assessment overload, staff workload and poor understanding among students and staff.

Why it matters

Anyone proposing a program-wide change should read the implementation problems before the principles.

Ways of thinking about AI and assessment

4 sources
Structural, not discursiveCorbin, Dawson and Liu, Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 2025Open ↗
What it argues

Separates discursive changes, which are instructions students can ignore, from structural changes that alter how the task actually works. It argues that traffic-light frameworks borrow the language of enforcement without any means of enforcing it.

Why it matters

This is why the repository sorts designs by what AI can and cannot do to the task, rather than relying on the level alone. A level on the Flinders AI Assessment Scale tells students what is permitted and what they must provide, and on an unsupervised task No AI permitted cannot be checked, so the repository does not offer it there.

Validity matters more than cheatingDawson, Bearman, Dollinger and Boud, Assessment and Evaluation in Higher Education, 2024Open ↗
What it argues

Argues that effort has gone into policing cheating when the real question is whether an assessment still supports a sound conclusion about what a student can do. The question shifts from whether AI use is cheating to whether it undermines the conclusion you want to draw.

Why it matters

Every design in the repository says what it can show about a student and where that evidence stops, in place of a claim that it is secure.

The AI Assessment ScalePerkins, Roe and Furze, 2025, revising Perkins, Furze, Roe and MacVaughOpen ↗
What it argues

A widely used scale for telling students how much AI use a task permits. The Flinders AI Assessment Scale is adapted from it and has six levels: No AI permitted, AI-Prompted, AI-Assisted Idea Generation & Structuring, AI-Assisted Editing, AI-Task Completion, Human Evaluation, and Full AI.

Why it matters

Each design in the repository suggests a level on the Flinders scale, says why, and gives the Flinders wording for students to use as written. The level stays your decision. The Using the AI Assessment Scale page sets out all six levels and how to acknowledge AI use.

Interactive oralsSotiriadou, Logan, Daly and Guest, Studies in Higher Education, 2020Open ↗
What it argues

Describes unscripted, real-time, two-way conversations built around a professional scenario, and distinguishes them from vivas, presentations and scripted question and answer. The study finds that tasks built around interactive orals help prevent misconduct, and that the closer an assessment sits to real workplace situations, the less incentive students have to cheat.

Why it matters

It is the research base for the most widely adopted design in the repository. Krautloher (2024, Journal of University Teaching and Learning Practice) adds that students from equity backgrounds reported a better assessment experience, though that finding rests on how students felt rather than on their results.
